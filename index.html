The Project

404: trad-designer is a digital experiment disguised as a brainstorming tool, but ultimately a space for feminist inquiry into product design.

This project was born from the anger of realizing that the everyday design world still bears the fingerprints of patriarchy. Every product holds untold stories of gender, labor, and power. This word generator exists to reveal those traces, empowering alternative thinking and creative resistance.

The website relies on a word-based archive system that allows for 2.29 billion possible combinations. It operates without any Western male-centered, biased AI, letting anyone blend design terminology with feminist vocabulary to generate speculative design prompts. 404: trad-designer is deliberately human, critical, and feminist.

Here, words mix, remix, blend, and collide, forming prompts that sound unexpected, sometimes unthinkable, and always political.

It's a free, open-source conceptual juxtaposition tool for preparing your own feminist design recipe, one word at a time, since the very beginning of the design process, whether you are creating or questioning a brief.

404: trad-designer doesn't promise perfect briefs or efficient solutions; it invites speculation, rebellion, and reconstruction.

Manifesto

1. Good design is transformative and intersectional.
Design challenges existing power structures and amplifies marginalized voices by addressing overlapping social identities and systemic inequalities, creating futures where diversity thrives.
2. Good design serves all users equally.
Design practices actively dismantle exclusionary norms by centering accessibility and inclusivity to ensure all bodies, identities, and abilities experience equitable benefit.
3. Good design is value-driven and free from gender biases.
Design rejects patriarchal assumptions and stereotypes, embedding feminist values of equity, care, and justice into the core of the design process and outcomes. It prioritizes function and value over aesthetic differentiation or superficial appeal.
4. Good design is transparent and benefit-focused
Design communicates openly about its intentions, impacts, and limitations while prioritizing the well-being and empowerment of people, ensuring that the design serves their needs ethically and respectfully.
5. Good design is anthropological
Design integrates a deep understanding of the cultural, historical, geographical, and social roots of the user, while consciously addressing the diverse biases present in both user and designer. It recognizes the complex social fabric shaping needs beyond normative narratives.
6. Good design is critical and reflexive.
Design operates as a continuous, self-reflective process, consistently scrutinizing its inherent biases, standpoint, and broader sociopolitical consequences. This iterative questioning promotes a culture of accountability and ongoing ethical reflection.
7. Good design is sustainable and systems-conscious.
Design embraces ecological and social sustainability, recognizing interdependence between human and non-human systems and advocating for regenerative futures.
8. Good design is not universal and not one-size-fits-all.
Design not only acknowledges, respects, and values difference, but also actively counteracts the data gaps resulting from the application of universal norms, such as the "Reference Man", which tend to erase or marginalize diverse identity markers.
9. Good design maximizes choice and adaptability.
Design empowers users by offering flexible, customizable, and personalized solutions that honor autonomy and evolving needs, rather than imposing rigid or prescriptive forms.
10. Good design is way more than just 10 principles.
Design is a dynamic and evolving practice, informed by ongoing feminist thought, community engagement, and the collective imagination of inclusive futures beyond fixed frameworks. In this sense, a feminist design manifesto cannot be reduced to a static set of ten principles; rather, it remains an ever-evolving and intangible constellation of feminist futures principles.

Toolbox

Since the toolboxes out there are mostly designed for alpha-male performativity, here is a toolbox that functions instead as an evolving, intangible container of feminist tools for design speculation. It transcends traditional technical methods by establishing epistemological and methodological frameworks that specifically address entrenched patriarchal and anthropocentric biases in design processes.

Critical Reflexivity and Bias Awareness

Designers should actively examine and discuss their own assumptions and those of their team to prevent the reinforcement of stereotypes and avoid unintentionally excluding users (Microsoft, 2025). Forms of exclusion based on factors like learning methods, physical abilities, cognitive styles, and existing social biases should be detected and taken into consideration (Microsoft, 2025). It’s also important to recognize and address the prevalence of male-centered data, especially when it results in gaps related to non-male experiences, and develop new standards using specialized research (Perez, 2019). Going beyond the “Reference Man” (Perez, 2019) is mandatory when designing.

Speculation Checkpoints:

  • Have I taken into account my own and my team’s biases?
  • Does this design rely on the “Reference Man”?
  • Have I checked, both within the team, the process, and the design result, for exclusion across physical capabilities, learning styles, and cognitive demands?

Sex and Gender Analysis

Integrate sex and gender analysis into the design process to encourage innovation, introduce new viewpoints, and ensure that solutions meet the needs of a broad range of gender expressions (Tannenbaum et al., 2019). Recognize patriarchal biases that are instilled, even if differently, both in men and women.

Speculation Checkpoints:

  • Have I discussed the gender composition of my team?
  • Am I reinforcing or challenging gender stereotypes?
  • Can the design truly be gender neutral?

Intersectionality Integration

Intersectional thinking should be embedded early in the design process (Jones et al., 2021). This approach considers how various aspects of identity, such as age, disability, education, ethnicity, family set-up, gender, geographical location, race, sex, sexuality, class, sustainability, appearance, language, political beliefs, religion, immigration background, work history, indigeneity, and health, overlap and interact. By doing so, it ensures that lived experiences are taken into account, allowing for nuanced and tailored solutions that address complex forms of diversity (Jones et al., 2021).

Speculation Checkpoints:

  • Have I integrated multiple intersecting factors into design thinking from the beginning?
  • Where does exclusion happen most acutely by analyzing the combined effect of intersecting factors?
  • Can I creatively combine intersecting factors to create new opportunities?

Valuing Situated and Embodied Knowledge

Treat personal experience as a legitimate and valued form of knowledge (Barbour, 2018, in Friis et al., 2024). In line with the feminist principle that “the personal is theoretical” (Ahmed, 2017, in Buckley, 2019: 4), design practice should move past abstract notions to recognize women as creators, users, consumers, and facilitators.

Speculation Checkpoints:

  • Are we actively recognizing users as the true experts on the barriers they face?
  • Am I taking into account the situated and embodied realities of women as producers, users, and consumers throughout the design?
  • Can I incorporate my own lived experience as valuable knowledge into the design process?

Focus on the Micro-Scale

Acknowledge both small- and large-scale perspectives, focusing particular attention on micro, domestic, intimate, and everyday spaces as key areas for meaningful intervention (Buckley, 2019). This involves acknowledging the significance of daily practices and the construction of ordinary material culture (Buckley, 2019).

Speculation Checkpoints:

  • Am I prioritizing small-scale contexts in my design?
  • Have I integrated everyday material culture and routine practices?
  • Am I balancing micro-level details with larger macro trends to guide effective design?

Co-Design and Participatory Processes

Integrate participatory methods to foster more democratic and inclusive approaches to shaping the future (Friis et al., 2024). This involves actively engaging with users and community members to understand their needs and perspectives, much like an ethnographer or anthropologist would. Make sure that these individuals play an active role in the co-design process and act as a mediator to encourage collaboration. This approach counters the myth of the lone, heroic, god-like creator (Buckley, 2019) by recognizing the collective efforts and contributions that make product creation possible (Friis et al., 2024: 9).

Speculation Checkpoints:

  • How can I allow full participation in the design process?
  • Have I become a mediator between users in the co-design process?
  • Have I become an ethnographer in my target community?

Centering Grassroots Agendas

Give precedence to projects that are initiated by grassroots organizations and community groups at the local level. By focusing on projects that emerge from within communities, the design process ensures that decisions are informed by those with first-hand knowledge and direct experience of the relevant challenges. This bottom-up approach helps validate local expertise and empowers communities to take an active role in shaping their own solutions (Friis et al., 2024).

Speculation Checkpoints:

  • Did the idea spark from the local grassroots?
  • Am I validating grassroots community know-how and wisdom?
  • Have I ensured that local voices and expertise drive decision-making throughout the project?

Making Labor Visible

It is essential in feminist practice and theory to emphasize the significance of labor, particularly work that is often overlooked and not financially compensated, such as caregiving typically performed by women. Additionally, recognize the contribution of anyone who made the project possible (Friis et al., 2024).

Speculation Checkpoints:

  • Do I value invisible labor?
  • Did I credit all contributors?
  • Is labor recognized and compensated?

Posthuman Vitalism

Critically challenge the Western anthropocentric ideal of the “Man of Reason,” which casts men as the ultimate standard and sole possessors of rationality, discipline, and progress (Braidotti, 2013). Uncover the hidden exclusions and hierarchies embedded in this supposed ideal, exposing its roots in white, Western, and colonial thought. Embrace process-oriented ontologies that emphasize the interconnectedness of human and non-human agents, and go beyond the limitations of social constructivism thinking (Braidotti, 2013).

Speculation Checkpoints:

  • Am I challenging anthropocentric design standards?
  • Did I generate any hierarchy?
  • Have I considered the influence of non-human forces?

Strategic Reframing for Sustainability

Combine strategic defamiliarization (Braidotti, 2013) with a commitment to long-term planetary sustainability by rejecting narrow and deterministic patterns of thinking that hinder social justice and ecological health. This means redefining issues to move away from rigid binaries (such as fixed gender roles) and understanding environmental care as a long-term economy (McCorduck & Ramsey, 1996, as cited in Milojević, 2024). Reframing requires consciously questioning the tendency to treat the future purely as a domain for planning and preparation (Miller, 2018).

Speculation Checkpoints:

  • Am I moving beyond deterministic ways of thinking?
  • Can I imagine future alternatives without relying only on what is probable?
  • Do my design choices promote long-term ecological and social sustainability?

Feminist Imaginaries and Futurism

Use speculative and critical design methods to build imagination infrastructures (Friis et al., 2024). The goal is to envision alternative, equitable futures and foster “muscle memory” (Friis et al., 2024: 3) of reimagining the world in new ways. Instead of using design solely to provide solutions, focus on raising new questions and fostering ontological expansion (Miller, 2018).

Speculation Checkpoints:

  • Has my design process generated new questions?
  • Can I create a design that explores possibilities beyond providing direct solutions?
  • Can I design a question?
